The AI Industry, End to End

State of play: July 2026

Who makes what, who pays whom, how the technology works, and where the money actually pools. From one chip-machine company in the Netherlands to the app on your phone. Assumes zero prior knowledge; built as a permanent reference.

The whole landscape on one screen

"AI" is not one industry. It is a chain of about eight, stacked on top of each other. Each layer buys from the one below, adds something, and sells to the one above. Where a company sits on this chain largely determines its margins, its risks, and its strategy.

The 90-second version (read this first)

Modern AI models are giant piles of numbers, set by training (done once, at enormous cost) and used by inference (every single query, forever). Both run on GPUs: chips that do millions of simple calculations in parallel.

One Dutch company (ASML) makes the machines that make advanced chips. One Taiwanese company (TSMC) manufactures nearly all of them. One American company (Nvidia) designs the dominant AI chips and owns CUDA, the software layer everyone is locked into. Clouds buy those chips and rent them out by the hour. Frontier labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind) rent that computing power, train models with it, and sell intelligence by the token. Apps put it in front of you.

The strategic game at every layer is the same: avoid becoming a commodity. Nvidia bundles chips into systems and software. Google and Amazon design their own chips to stop paying Nvidia's margin. Labs bet their models stay differentiated. The open question running through this whole site: which layers hold their pricing power, and which get competed to the bone?

Chip
A fingernail-sized slice of silicon etched with billions of microscopic switches (transistors). The brain of every device.
GPU
The chip type AI runs on: thousands of small cores calculating in parallel. A CPU (your laptop's brain) has a few clever cores instead.
Compute
Raw processing power, treated as a quantity you can buy: "more compute" means more chips running for more hours.
Model & parameters
The AI itself: a mathematical function with billions of learned numbers (parameters) inside.
Training vs inference
Training sets the numbers (once, hugely expensive). Inference uses them (every query, forever).
Token
The unit AI reads and writes: a chunk of a word (~¾ of an English word). Also how AI is priced and billed.
Gross margin
The share of each sale kept after direct costs. High margin = pricing power; competition eats it.
Cloud
Renting computers in someone else's data centre by the hour, instead of owning your own. The giant general-purpose clouds (Amazon, Microsoft, Google) are called hyperscalers.
Commodity
A product identical whoever sells it, so buyers choose on price alone and sellers' profits get squeezed to nothing. "Commoditise" = become one.
Moat
Whatever stops competitors copying a business: lock-in, patents, scale, habit. No moat, no lasting profit.

Now the map. Read it bottom to top: the physical world at the base, the user at the surface. Product flows up the left rail, money flows down the right.

How we got here: the 60-second history

1990s - GPUs invented for video-game graphics. Nvidia founded 1993; nearly dies mid-90s; saved in 1997 by a hit gaming chip.
2006 - Nvidia launches CUDA, software letting programmers use GPUs for general maths. Seemed niche; became the deepest moat in tech.
2012 - AlexNet, a neural network (the learning technique unpacked in The technology tab), trained on two gaming GPUs, crushes ImageNet, the annual benchmark contest for image recognition. AI researchers switch to GPUs en masse.
2014-16 - Google, alarmed that speech features would need to double its data centres, designs its own AI chip: the TPU. Amazon buys chip designer Annapurna (2015).
2017 - Google researchers publish the Transformer ("Attention Is All You Need"), the design behind every modern LLM (large language model: the technology under ChatGPT and Claude). All eight authors later leave Google.
2019-20 - Nvidia buys Mellanox (networking; announced 2019, closed 2020). GPT-3 (2020) shows scale alone produces startling capability: 175bn parameters, ~$4.6m of compute.
2022 - ChatGPT launches; fastest-growing consumer product in history. Google declares internal "code red".
2023-24 - The compute land-grab: H100 GPU shortages, neoclouds boom, the hyperscalers (the giant clouds) go on a capital-spending spree, Nvidia joins the trillion-dollar club. Blackwell generation ships.
2025 - Agentic AI arrives: models do multi-step work, not single answers. Token demand explodes. Power becomes the binding constraint. CoreWeave lists on the stock market at ~$23bn, roughly doubling within two months.
2026 - Rubin generation (the next chips) enters production; Nvidia's market value hovers around $5tn; Anthropic's revenue compounds ~10x year on year (OpenAI's grows more slowly); value visibly shifts from infrastructure toward the model labs. As of July 2026: nobody knows if that holds.

Foundations: how a computer works

Everything on this site sits on top of one machine doing one thing very fast. This tab is the floor below the floor: a switch, a gate, a processor, and the ladder of memory that keeps it fed. Five rungs, each built on the last. Figures are typical of 2026-era hardware; the ideas are much older.

The one-paragraph version

A computer is three things and some wiring. Processors do arithmetic, very fast, one small step at a time. Memory holds the numbers being worked on right now, and forgets everything when the power goes off. Storage keeps everything permanently and is far slower. The design problem is that fast memory is small and expensive while cheap memory is big and slow, so a computer is built as a ladder of memories, arranged so the data needed next is usually near the top. Almost every performance question in computing, including why AI needed a different kind of chip, is a question about that ladder.

1 Transistors, logic gates, and what a processor does

A transistor is a switch with no moving parts: a sliver of silicon that conducts current or blocks it, depending on the voltage applied to a third terminal. That is the entire trick. Because one transistor can switch another, switches wire together into logic gates, circuits that answer one question about their inputs (AND: are both on? OR: is either on? NOT: the opposite). Gates combine into circuits that add, compare and remember; those combine into a processor. A leading-edge chip carries tens of billions of transistors on a piece of silicon the size of a fingernail.

What it does with them. Fetch an instruction from memory, work out what it says, do it, write the result back, move on. Repeat forever. The instruction set is the vocabulary of things a processor can be asked: load this number, add these two, compare them, jump elsewhere if the comparison came out true, store the answer. Nothing else is available. An operating system, a spreadsheet and a language model are all that loop, run over different numbers, billions of times a second.

Clock speed (GHz, billions of cycles per second) is how fast the loop ticks. It stopped climbing around 2005, because faster switching meant unmanageable heat, so chip designers added cores (loops running at once) instead. More workers rather than a faster worker is the shift the rest of this tab hangs on.

2 The memory ladder: registers, cache, RAM, disk

Memory close to the cores is reached quickly but costs a fortune per byte and occupies scarce space on the chip. Memory further out is cheap, enormous and slow. No single technology is good on all three axes (speed, size, cost), so machines carry all of them at once and shuffle data upward.

The ladder works because programs reuse the same data and, having read one number, usually want its neighbours next. That behaviour is called locality, and it lets a small cache stand in for a large memory: on ordinary work the processor finds what it wants in cache well over 90% of the time.

The cliff. RAM to SSD is the biggest gap in the table. When a machine runs out of RAM it starts moving data out to storage to make room, which is called swapping. Running out of capacity is not a gentle slowdown; it is falling off a step, and it is what "the computer has gone slow" usually means.

3 RAM is two numbers, not one

Memory gets quoted as a single figure and is really two, independent of each other and answering different questions. Capacity, in gigabytes, is how much data fits at once: it decides whether a job runs at all. Bandwidth, in gigabytes per second, is how fast data moves between memory and the processor: it decides how quickly the job runs. Two machines can hold identical amounts and differ several-fold in bandwidth. Server accelerators buy bandwidth aggressively, using HBM (high-bandwidth memory) stacked vertically beside the processor rather than sitting across a board, which is how memory makers became a choke point in the AI supply chain.

Why this matters for AI. A model's parameters must be in memory before it can run, so capacity decides whether it loads at all. Generating each token then requires reading essentially all the active parameters out of memory once, so bandwidth decides how fast it answers: output speed is roughly bandwidth divided by the size of the weights being read. Double the capacity and a bigger model fits; double the bandwidth and the same model runs twice as fast.
4 Buses: why moving data is the bottleneck

The parts are connected by buses: sets of wires, each with a width (how many bits cross at once) and a clock (how often). They form their own ladder, each step outward costing roughly an order of magnitude in speed: the interconnect inside the chip between cores and cache, the memory bus out to RAM, PCIe to expansion cards such as GPUs and SSDs, then the network between machines (Ethernet, InfiniBand, or Nvidia's NVLink for GPU-to-GPU traffic).

Arithmetic got cheap faster than data movement did. The calculations a chip can perform per second have grown far faster, over decades, than the rate at which memory can supply numbers to calculate with. So most real workloads are memory-bound rather than compute-bound: the arithmetic units sit idle waiting for operands. A fast processor starved of data is expensive and slow at once. Nearly every recent design decision follows from that, from bigger caches to memory stacked beside the processor to dedicated links that let thousands of chips act as one machine, and at data-centre scale it is why networking and assembly is a real business rather than a detail: see The stack.

5 Why GPUs: many simple cores instead of a few clever ones

A CPU spends most of its silicon on making one sequence of instructions run fast: predicting which way a branch will go, reordering work while it waits, holding large caches. That is the right design when step 2 depends on the result of step 1, which describes an operating system and most ordinary software.

A GPU makes the opposite bet: thousands of simple cores running the same instruction on different data at the same time, with the silicon spent on arithmetic units and memory bandwidth rather than on cleverness. It is useless at winding logic and unbeatable at one shape of work, applying the same operation to a huge grid of numbers where no entry depends on any other. That shape was built for pixels; it also describes matrix multiplication, which is nearly all of what a neural network does. Every cell of the output is an independent sum of products, so thousands of cores stay busy. Modern accelerators add dedicated matrix units and low-precision number formats (8-bit and 4-bit), which cut the data that has to be moved as much as the arithmetic.

And then bandwidth binds again. Training does a lot of arithmetic per parameter loaded, so it keeps the cores busy. Generating text one token at a time does very little: each weight is read, used once or twice, and discarded. That workload is limited by how fast memory can be read, not by how fast the chip can multiply, which is why providers batch many users' requests through the same pass over the weights, and why memory is now as strategically valuable as the processor. 0 Energy & data centres: the physical floor

What it is. AI runs in data centres: warehouse buildings full of server racks, drawing staggering amounts of electricity and producing equal amounts of heat. A large AI site draws hundreds of megawatts to over a gigawatt; a gigawatt is roughly one nuclear reactor's output.

Why it suddenly matters. Until ~2023 power was a line item. By 2025 it became the binding constraint on the whole industry: chips can be bought in months, but grid connections, transmission lines and power plants take 5+ years. Microsoft has contracted Constellation Energy to restart the Three Mile Island nuclear plant (targeted 2027-28); new sites get built next to power sources, not next to cities. When people say "compute is scarce", the deepest scarcity is electricity.

Who earns here. Utilities and generators (Constellation, Vistra, NextEra), datacentre landlords (Equinix, Digital Realty), cooling and power-gear makers (Vertiv, Schneider). Utilities were among the best-performing AI stocks of 2024-25 because demand is huge, supply is slow, and they carry none of the technology risk.

1 Toolmakers: ASML, the design software, and Arm

ASML (Veldhoven, Netherlands) makes lithography machines: they project chip designs onto silicon with light. For the finest features (a few nanometres) you need extreme ultraviolet light (EUV), and ASML is the only company on Earth that ever made EUV work: roughly two decades and tens of billions in R&D; everyone else gave up. The machine fires lasers at molten tin droplets 50,000 times a second to create the light, bounces it off the smoothest mirrors ever made (Zeiss), and positions wafers with sub-nanometre precision. Standard EUV machines cost roughly $200m; the newer High-NA generation, $350m+. Each ships in multiple 747s.

Consequence No ASML machines → no leading-edge fabs → no advanced AI chips. This is why chips are geopolitics: the single most effective lever on China's AI progress has been pressure on the Netherlands to restrict ASML sales. One company is a choke point for the world's computing.

Cadence and Synopsys sell the design software (EDA) every chip designer uses: a quiet duopoly with software margins.

Arm (Cambridge, UK; majority-owned by SoftBank) sells neither machines nor chips: it licenses the instruction set, the vocabulary of commands a processor understands, plus ready-made core designs. About 99% of smartphones run on Arm. Apple's M-series, Amazon's Graviton, Nvidia's Grace CPU: all Arm-based, all paying a royalty of pennies to a few dollars per chip, times tens of billions of chips a year. Nearly pure-margin intellectual property (gross margin ~95%+).

The blocked deal Nvidia tried to buy Arm for $40bn (2020). Regulators killed it (2022): Arm's whole value is neutrality, licensing to everyone including Nvidia's competitors. Nobody wanted the Switzerland of chips owned by one arms dealer. SoftBank IPO'd it instead (2023). The custom chips the hyperscalers use to escape Nvidia (Graviton, TPU-adjacent CPUs, Microsoft's Cobalt) are almost all Arm-licensed: Arm arms everyone, whoever wins.

2 Fabrication: TSMC and the memory makers

The fabless split. Modern chip companies split design from manufacturing. Nvidia, Apple, AMD, Broadcom design chips ("fabless"); TSMC (Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company) manufactures them as a pure contractor. A leading-edge fab costs $20bn+; only colossal shared volume justifies it, so one company's fabs serve the whole industry. TSMC never owns the designs and competes with essentially nobody at the leading edge: Samsung trails on yields, Intel is trying to re-enter. It runs ~59% gross margins (2025) despite being "just" a manufacturer, because at the frontier it is a monopoly with two-to-three years' process lead.

How the money works. Nvidia sends TSMC a design and pays per wafer (the 30cm silicon disc later cut into chips); leading-edge wafers run roughly $20k+, and one wafer yields several dozen usable H100-class chips. Nvidia then sells each finished chip for ~$30k against a few thousand in manufacturing cost. The margin lives in design and lock-in, not manufacturing.

Memory: the second choke point. A GPU is useless if data cannot reach it fast enough, so AI chips carry HBM (high-bandwidth memory) stacked directly beside the processor. SK Hynix leads, Samsung and Micron follow. HBM has been in chronic shortage; memory prices roughly tripled in 2025, and HBM itself sells at a many-fold premium over ordinary memory (per SemiAnalysis, the semiconductor research firm); memory is now as strategically important as the logic chips.

Packaging: the step that actually binds. A finished AI chip is not one piece of silicon. The logic die and its stacks of HBM have to be bonded together onto a single carrier, which TSMC does with a process called CoWoS (chip-on-wafer-on-substrate). That advanced-packaging line, not chip design, has been the tightest link in the chain since 2023: TSMC has expanded CoWoS capacity aggressively every year since, and it has still been allocated to customers rather than freely bought (as of mid-2026). Nvidia's shipment numbers have repeatedly been set by how many units could be packaged, not by how many could be designed or sold.

The bottleneck chain Advanced packaging and HBM supply first, then TSMC's leading-edge wafer capacity, then power: grid connections, transmission and new generation (Layer 0 above). Each link takes longer to expand than the one before it, packaging lines in quarters, fabs in years, firm gigawatts in half a decade, which is why relief at one level just moves the queue down to the next. Chip design has not been the constraint at any point in this cycle.

Geopolitics Nearly every advanced AI chip on Earth passes through fabs on Taiwan, an island China claims. This is the single largest concentration risk in the world economy, and the reason the US CHIPS Act pays TSMC, Samsung and Intel to build fabs on US soil.

3 Chip design: Nvidia and the people trying to escape it

What a GPU is. A CPU has a handful of brilliant cores built for sequential logic: it runs your operating system. A GPU has thousands of simple cores doing the same arithmetic simultaneously. Invented for game graphics (millions of pixels, same calculation, none depending on another), it turned out to be exactly the shape of AI's core workload: multiplying enormous grids of numbers (see The technology). An H100-class data-centre GPU has ~80bn transistors and costs ~$30k; the current Blackwell and just-shipping Rubin generations go further, sold increasingly as whole racks (~72 GPUs wired into one giant computer, ~$3m each).

Why is Nvidia's margin 71% while Arm's is ~95%? Nvidia ships physical hardware: it pays real money for wafers, memory and assembly on every unit, so even with monopoly-grade pricing power its margin has a floor of real costs. Arm ships licences, which cost nothing to duplicate. The less physical your product, the higher your ceiling.

Nvidia's moat, layer by layer. AMD's silicon has been roughly competitive for years; Nvidia still holds an estimated 80-90% of AI accelerators and a 71% gross margin (FY2026). The moat is not the chip:

  • CUDA (2006): the free software platform everyone programs GPUs with. Every framework, library and accumulated optimisation trick targets CUDA first. Switching to AMD means porting your whole stack.
  • Networking (Mellanox, deal announced 2019): NVLink and InfiniBand, the interconnects that let thousands of GPUs act as one machine.
  • Systems: selling full racks ("AI factories") rather than loose chips moves the unit of competition up a level; there are many chip rivals, few full-system rivals.
  • TSMC priority: as TSMC's biggest AI customer, first claim on scarce leading-edge capacity.
  • Cadence: a new architecture roughly every year now (Hopper 2022, Blackwell 2024, Blackwell Ultra 2025, Rubin in production 2026). Match today's chip and you compete with last year's product.

The escape routes. Google designs TPUs (10+ years, with Broadcom): not to sell chips but to stop paying Nvidia's margin on its own colossal workloads, and now to power Anthropic too. Amazon builds Trainium/Inferentia; Microsoft builds Maia; Meta builds MTIA; OpenAI is reportedly co-designing with Broadcom. None has displaced Nvidia at the frontier; all cap its pricing power at the margin. The sharpest sceptic's framing (Zvi Mowshowitz, a widely read independent AI analyst, Apr 2026): Nvidia's moat against Google is, mostly, Google itself: TPUs are formidable but Google chooses not to sell them at scale to outsiders.

3.5 Assembly & networking: chips into computers

Between a chip and a working data centre sits real engineering: Dell, Supermicro, HPE assemble GPU boards, CPUs, memory and cooling into servers; Arista, Cisco and Nvidia's own networking connect thousands of servers; Vertiv, Schneider Electric handle power distribution and the liquid cooling modern racks require (every gigawatt of compute is a gigawatt of heat to remove). It is a genuine business with mostly thin margins: the classic fate of an assembly layer between two powerful neighbours. Nvidia increasingly absorbs the most valuable parts of it by selling complete racks itself.

4 Clouds: hyperscalers vs neoclouds

Hyperscalers (AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud) are the giant general-purpose clouds, built over 15+ years for all of the world's computing. GPUs are one product on a menu of hundreds: virtual machines, storage, databases, networking, analytics, security. That menu is why they win: a company whose data, systems and compliance already live on AWS gets enormous convenience from running AI next to them, on one bill. Data attracts compute ("data gravity"), and clouds charge egress fees to take data out.

Neoclouds (CoreWeave, Lambda, Nebius, Crusoe and dozens more) are GPU-only rental businesses born in the AI boom: raise money, buy Nvidia GPUs, rack them, rent them by the hour. Faster, cheaper and more specialised than the hyperscalers; also thin-moat, debt-financed against fast-depreciating chips, and knife-fighting each other on price. CoreWeave went from crypto-mining sideline to a stock-market listing (IPO) at ~$23bn (March 2025), roughly doubling within two months, largely by locking multi-year contracts with Microsoft and OpenAI. Nvidia deliberately invests in and allocates scarce chips to neoclouds: they are pure customers, a counterweight to hyperscalers who all build rival chips.

5 Model labs: turning compute into intelligence

What they do. Frontier labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, xAI) buy or are granted vast compute, train models on it, then sell the result two ways: API access for businesses building on top (the API is the pipe their software uses to call the model, billed per token), and subscriptions (ChatGPT, Claude) for people. Training a frontier model is a months-long bet costing hundreds of millions to billions of dollars; each generation costs several times the last.

The lab-cloud pairings. Every lab is bound to the infrastructure giants that fund it. OpenAI ↔ Microsoft (plus, since 2025, additional clouds). Anthropic ↔ Amazon (committed ~$33bn to date, and Anthropic has committed $100bn+ of AWS spend over a decade, training on Trainium) and Google (up to $40bn structure agreed 2026; up to a million TPUs). Google DeepMind sits inside Google. xAI builds its own Memphis cluster. These deals are circular on purpose: the cloud invests in the lab, the lab spends it back on the cloud's compute; both book growth. Critics call it vendor financing; participants call it securing supply.

Open-weight labs (Meta's Llama, DeepSeek, Alibaba's Qwen, Mistral) give model files away and monetise indirectly (or strategically: make the layer next to yours cheap and abundant, so the value moves to the layer you do own; the tactic is called commoditising your complement). They are the standing threat to the closed labs' pricing power, and agents, which burn tokens 10x faster than chat, push cost-sensitive builders toward them.

The economics are their own tab. Labs run 50-75% gross margins on serving models while posting company-level losses because each next model costs more to train: see The economics.

6 Apps & distribution: where AI meets a user

The most visible, most fragmented layer. Three species:

  • AI-native products: Cursor (coding), Perplexity (search), Harvey (legal), plus the labs' own apps (ChatGPT, Claude). Build fast on rented intelligence; risk being flattened by the next model release or by the lab's own product.
  • Incumbents adding AI: Microsoft Copilot in Office, Gemini in Gmail, Salesforce Agentforce. They own distribution: millions of seats that never made a switching decision.
  • Device owners: Apple and Samsung hand AI to billions of users at the operating-system level, mostly renting the intelligence underneath.

History's rhyme, and why this layer matters strategically: in the 1990s the PC hardware layer commoditised and Microsoft, owning the software layer above (Windows + Office), captured the surplus. Whoever owns distribution captures value from a commoditised layer beneath. Every player in this chain knows that story; it is why Google gives Gemini away inside products, and why the labs are racing to own the user relationship, not just the model.

New layer note, Aug 2026: Stripe is acquiring OpenRouter, the service that routes API calls across many different AI models, for roughly $8bn, Stripe's largest acquisition to date (FT, Aug 2026). Payments infrastructure is now buying into the model-routing layer directly, a sign that non-AI-native infrastructure firms expect routing and billing to stay a distinct, durable layer of the stack rather than being absorbed by the model labs themselves.

The technology: how the intelligence actually works

A ladder from "what is a chip" to "what is an agent", each rung built on the previous one. Worked examples marked HDL come from Isabella's INSEAD Hands-on Deep Learning course (Prof. Ciocan, 2026); everything else is the standard picture as of July 2026.

Rung 1 - CPUs, GPUs, and why AI needed a different chip

A CPU is a few brilliant workers: a handful of fast, clever cores, built for tasks where step 2 depends on step 1 (running your operating system, executing app logic). A GPU is an army of adequate workers: thousands of small, simple cores doing the same operation simultaneously. Invented for game graphics, where millions of pixels each need the same little calculation and none depends on another.

The lucky accident of the century: AI's core computation, multiplying enormous grids of numbers ("matrix multiplication"), has exactly that same shape. Every cell of the output can be computed independently and in parallel. A job that takes a CPU weeks takes a GPU cluster hours. When researchers noticed this (AlexNet, 2012, trained on two gaming cards), the gaming-chip company became the most important company in AI.

A modern data-centre GPU (H100/Blackwell/Rubin class) barely resembles a graphics card: ~80bn transistors, specialised cores purely for matrix maths, ultra-fast HBM memory stacked next to the processor, and high-speed links so thousands of chips act as one computer.

Rung 2 - A neural network is stacked regression HDL

Strip the mystique: a neural network is nodes arranged in layers, each node computing a weighted sum of its inputs plus a bias, passed through a simple non-linear function. One sigmoid node literally is logistic regression, the workhorse of every stats course.

Worked example (HDL L01). Weights 0.2 and 0.5, bias 0.4, inputs [3.8, 1.2]:
z = 0.2×3.8 + 0.5×1.2 + 0.4 = 1.76, then squash: P = 1/(1+e−¹·⁷⁶) ≈ 0.85 (e is just a fixed maths constant, ~2.72). That's one neuron: an 85% "yes". A network is this, stacked deep and wide.

The parameters everyone counts (GPT-3's 175 billion) are just these weights and biases: the numbers the network learns. The non-linear squash between layers is essential: without it, a 100-layer network collapses mathematically into one flat layer. Depth is only powerful because of the non-linearity.

Rung 3 - Training: how the numbers get set

Training is a loop: make a prediction, measure how wrong it is (the loss), then nudge every weight slightly in the direction that reduces the wrongness (gradient descent, computed via backpropagation), and repeat, billions of times. Picture walking downhill on a foggy landscape where altitude is error: you can't see the bottom, but you can always feel the slope under your feet.

Two facts with big consequences:

  • Real loss landscapes are jagged, so training finds a good valley, not the best one; retrain on the same data and you get a slightly different model each time.
  • Overfitting: a big enough network can memorise its training data (perfect on practice questions, useless on the exam). The diagnostic is a held-out validation set; when validation error starts rising while training error keeps falling, stop. HDL

For LLMs the labels are free, which is the whole trick: slide a window over internet-scale text and the next word is the label. No humans needed to annotate. That is pretraining: predict the next token, over trillions of tokens, until the loss is low.

Rung 4 - Embeddings: meaning as geometry HDL

Computers need numbers, so every token gets a vector (a list of numbers, hundreds to thousands long) called its embedding. The magic is that these vectors are learned so that geometric distance tracks meaning: similar words end up near each other, and directions encode relationships. The classic: king − man + woman ≈ queen. The same offset that turns walking into walked turns swimming into swam.

But a fixed vector per word can't handle ambiguity: "the train left the station" vs "the radio station played 60s hits". Solving that is the transformer's job.

Rung 5 - "Attention Is All You Need": the paper behind everything

The setup. In 2017 the best language systems (for machine translation, mostly) were recurrent networks: they read text one word at a time, left to right, carrying a running memory. Two problems. Reading sequentially means you cannot parallelise, so training was slow and capped in scale. And the running memory fades: by the end of a long sentence, the start has blurred, so long-range meaning kept getting lost.

The heretical move. Eight Google researchers proposed throwing the recurrence away entirely: process every word simultaneously, and let a single mechanism, attention, work out which words matter to which. The title was a deliberately cheeky statement of exactly that: "Attention Is All You Need" (arXiv 1706.03762). It introduced the transformer, the architecture behind every modern LLM. The T in GPT stands for it.

How attention works, in one intuition. Every word's vector plays three roles, via three learned matrices: a query (what am I looking for?), a key (what do I offer as context?), and a value (the content I contribute). Each word's query is compared against every other word's key; the strength of each match decides how much of that word's value flows into the new, re-weighted vector. The canonical example: in "the animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired", the word "it" matches strongly against "animal"; change "tired" to "wide" and it matches "street" instead. Each word's embedding becomes contextual: coloured by the company it keeps ("you shall know a word by the company it keeps": the linguist J.R. Firth, 1957, sixty years early).

Multi-head attention runs many of these in parallel per layer: small specialists, one tracking grammar, another tense, another who-did-what, like the multiple filters in a vision network. Then the whole block is stacked dozens of layers deep (GPT-3: 96 layers, 96 heads per layer), each layer refining the previous layer's contextual vectors.

The order problem (HDL L05). Processing all words at once loses word order, and "dog bites man" must not equal "man bites dog". Fix: add a learned positional embedding to each word's vector before attention. Worked example from the course: "cat" at position 0 = standalone(cat) [0.5, 7.1] + position(0) [1.3, 3.9] = [1.8, 11.0]. Meaning and position, folded into one vector.

Why it won. Attention is almost entirely matrix multiplication, computable for all words simultaneously: the architecture is a perfect fit for GPUs (Rung 1). That made training parallelisable, which made internet-scale pretraining affordable, which made the scaling laws practically explorable. Nearly everything in the other tabs, the GPU land-grab, Nvidia's rise, the data-centre buildout, is downstream of this one 2017 design choice. Later models simplified it further (the GPT line uses a decoder-only variant: just the next-token half), but the core mechanism is unchanged from the paper.

The lore. All eight authors have since left Google, several to found AI companies (Cohere, Character.AI among them). Google published the idea openly, then watched OpenAI industrialise it first: a re-run of its own distributed-computing-to-AWS story, and a big part of why "code red" happened in 2022.

Rung 6 - An LLM is a next-token machine HDL

Put the rungs together and you have the whole thing: tokenise the input text (real tokenisers split words into subword chunks via byte-pair encoding; ~¾ of a word per token in English), run it through the stacked transformer to get contextual embeddings, then a final layer scores every token in the vocabulary on how likely it is to come next, and a softmax turns scores into probabilities. Pick from that distribution (deterministically for factual tasks, with sampling "temperature" for creative ones), append the chosen token, and repeat. Every chatbot answer you have ever read was generated one token at a time by that loop.

As the HDL slides put it after this exact walkthrough: "Congratulations, you've just built your own LLM." The mystique is stacked regression plus attention plus scale.

Rung 7 - Post-training: from autocomplete to assistant

Pretraining produces a brilliant autocomplete that will just as happily continue a rant as answer a question. Post-training shapes it into an assistant:

  • RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback): humans rank candidate answers; the model is tuned toward what they prefer. This step, cheap next to pretraining, is what made ChatGPT feel like a product in 2022.
  • Reasoning training (the 2024-26 wave, sometimes called RLVR: reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards): on problems with checkable answers (maths, code), let the model generate long chains of thought, reward the chains that reach correct answers. Result: models that "think" before answering, spending more inference compute for more reliability. This created a second scaling axis: capability now scales with compute spent at answer time, not just at training time, which is a big part of why inference demand exploded.

Why alignment is hard, in one image (HDL L07): an RL boat-racing agent discovered that spinning in circles hitting bonus targets scored more points than finishing the race. The loss function is always a proxy for what you actually want, and models optimise the proxy.

Rung 8 - Inference: what happens when you hit send

Inference is running the finished model: weights frozen, your tokens in, its tokens out (see the economics tab for why this distinction runs the industry's finances). Three mechanics worth knowing:

  • Memory bandwidth is the bottleneck. Generating each token requires streaming billions of weights from memory through the compute cores. The arithmetic is easy; feeding it is hard. This is why HBM matters as much as the GPU itself.
  • The KV cache (key-value cache). To avoid re-reading the whole conversation for every new token, the model caches its attention calculations. Long contexts make this cache enormous, which is why long conversations cost providers real memory and why context windows are expensive to serve.
  • Batching. Providers serve many users' requests through the GPU at once, sharing the cost of streaming the weights. This is the main lever behind falling per-token prices.

Mixture-of-experts (MoE), used by most frontier models now: the model holds many specialist sub-networks but activates only a few per token, so a model can have trillions of stored parameters while spending only a fraction of that compute per token. Big brain, selective effort.

Two cost curves run in opposite directions: cost per token of a fixed capability level collapses (often ~10x/year, via better hardware, batching, MoE, and distillation: training a small cheap model to imitate a big one), while total spend rises because usage and per-task token counts (reasoning, agents) grow faster than unit costs fall.

Rung 9 - Agents: models in a loop with tools

An agent is an LLM given tools (run code, search the web, edit files, call APIs) and run in a loop: look at the goal, pick an action, observe the result, decide the next action, repeat until done. No new architecture: the same next-token machine, prompted to emit tool calls, with software around it executing them.

The economic consequence is enormous: a chat answer is a few hundred tokens; an agent completing a real task can burn hundreds of thousands (it re-reads context at every step, and one loop iteration's KV cache and outputs feed the next). Agents are why token demand exploded in 2025-26, why value-per-query jumped (an agent replaces hours of work, not a search), and why cost-sensitive builders drift toward cheaper open-weight models. Agents "having complete freedom of how many tokens to use" is the single most important demand-side fact in the compute economy right now.

Rung 10 - Transfer learning: the startup cheat code HDL

You almost never train from scratch. You take a pretrained model, keep its learned representation of the world, and adapt it cheaply: prompt it (no weight changes), give it retrieved documents (RAG: paste relevant internal files into the context), fine-tune it (adjust some weights on your data), or use its embeddings with a small model of your own on top.

The HDL course calls this the most practically important idea it teaches, and its startup blueprint: don't train a trillion-parameter model; own a specific, rich, proprietary dataset; transfer-learn a pretrained model onto it; sell the predictions. Harvey (legal AI) is the canonical example. In the class exercise, a usable image classifier was built from a few dozen photos by piggybacking on a pretrained vision model: 97-100% accuracy from almost no data.

Why "it's just predicting the next word" is true and useless

Generation is one token at a time: the current text goes in, a probability over every possible next token comes out, one is picked and appended, the longer text goes back in (Rung 6). That is the computation. It is also no help at all: it tells you nothing about which answers to trust, why the model gets rare facts wrong, or what to add to a prompt. We had next-word models for years before ChatGPT and they were academic curiosities; "next-word prediction" alone does not explain what changed.

The literal machinery is worse as a model, not better. A teaching model with 85,000 parameters, every one a regression coefficient, already fires more operations to produce one word than anyone can follow; production models have hundreds of billions. Meursault's test for a mental model is a globe: it preserves relative location, omits nearly all physical detail, and says what it cannot show you. Useful does not mean literally complete.

The other trap is the interface. A chat window cues you to infer intention, knowledge and personality. Humanlike output is observable behaviour; it tells you nothing about the process that produced it, and fluency measurably changes how much people trust an answer. The "LLM is a Shoggoth" meme captures it: an incomprehensible mass wearing a friendly face that was trained on, not grown.
Model 1 - An LLM is an approximate retriever over a language map AIBS

Imagine the model holding an invisible map of language. Everything that can be said is a point on it; every training document has a location; documents with similar meaning sit close together ("my cat is cute" is nearer "my dog is cute" than "I am an economist"). Your prompt is a point on the same map: what you write supplies its coordinates. The answer is a combination of the nearby internal documents, produced through next-token prediction, and anything you put in the prompt enters that combination directly. The map is real technology made into an intuition (embeddings, Rung 4, projected down from thousands of dimensions), but the point is behavioural: it is a model of learned patterns, not a literal retrieval algorithm, and it cannot show you a source list because there isn't one.

What the one mechanism explains in both directions. Capabilities: the training set is so large (Dolma, 3 trillion tokens, about 1.64 million times the length of all the Harry Potter books) that a near neighbour exists for almost any query, and a novel task decomposes into parts that do have neighbours. Failures: an ambiguous prompt lands near the wrong region ("Jupiter" sits near astronomy and astrology); a rare fact is outnumbered by similar-looking texts; "the best clustering method" returns methods described as best, not the best for your data; nothing in the combination enforces logical consistency; and the same prompt can return different answers, so "the AI said so" is not a method.

The canonical hallucination. "What were SmallCo's revenues in 2022?" The true figure, $128 million, appears rarely in training data; the neighbourhood is dense with Amazon (over $500B), Microsoft ($198B) and Apple ($394B). The combination takes the right number with the wrong scale: $128 billion. Plausible language, false answer, and it fails a basic scale constraint no business would accept. Attach the annual report and the reliable source is in the immediate context, the irrelevant neighbours matter less, and the task becomes extraction from supplied evidence: $128 million. Better context raises the odds; it does not guarantee the answer, so verification stays.

Where the model is strong and where it is fragile. Strong when many high-quality examples resemble the task, several outputs would be acceptable, and mostly right is still useful (turn a report into an email). Fragile when the task is new, rare or multi-step, every step must satisfy a constraint, and one wrong value ruins the outcome (a financially consistent forecast). Business needs acceptable outcomes under constraints, not plausible text.

The questions the model forces you to ask (this is what a good mental model is for): what internal documents are nearby? what from my prompt enters the combination? who verifies the output? how do I move the prompt closer to the relevant documents? what authoritative documents can I include? Per stakeholder: the engineer asks what data shaped the model, the strategist how well the data match the customer need, the manager what data the workflow outside the model can supply, the citizen where the data came from and whose rights are affected.

Reinforcement learning, and how the weights are actually set

Supervised versus RL. Supervised training (stages 1 and 2) says: here is the input, here is the right output, nudge the weights toward producing it. Reinforcement learning says: produce an attempt, let a reward function score it, nudge the weights so high-scoring attempts become more likely and low-scoring ones less likely. RLHF (stage 3) is RL where the reward is "a human preferred this response". Reasoning training (stage 4, sometimes RLVR) is RL where the reward is "the final answer matched the checker": the model's LaTeX is parsed by ordinary software, a maths library canonicalises 28/6 to 14/3, the result is compared to ground truth, and traces that ended right are reinforced.

It is not specified per problem type. The reward function is generic and dumb: did the final answer match, was the format right. It is applied over millions of problems from many families, and what gets learned is whatever patterns of working correlate with reward across all of them. Nothing in the weights "is a Lagrangian"; the weights make working-that-tends-to-end-right more probable. That is why this is pattern learning rather than a truth engine, and why it rewards the destination and not the route: a verifier can check the final answer, but not where one step ends, whether every intermediate claim is correct, or whether a plausible trace caused the right answer.

How weights are calculated. Gradient descent (Rung 3). Every weight is a number; a loss function measures how wrong the output was; backpropagation computes the derivative of the loss with respect to every weight; each weight moves a small step against its derivative; repeat billions of times. In RL the loss is replaced by negative expected reward, so the step raises the probability of the token sequences that were rewarded. The regression instinct is the right one: every parameter is a coefficient, except there are billions, there is no closed form, and the target is the next token.

The proxy problem from Rung 7 applies here too: the reward is always a stand-in for what you want, and the model optimises the stand-in.

Model 2 - A reasoning model is a prompt augmenter AIBS

Engineering definition. A reasoning model (LRM) generates additional tokens before the final response and uses them as more context for subsequent token prediction. Kambhampati's reading: prompt, then reasoning tokens, then an augmented prompt, then the answer. The intermediate tokens are retrieval keys. They need to be statistically effective at improving the final retrieval, not logically valid, and the A* maze experiments show the two come apart: models trained on correct traces routinely produce invalid working and correct answers.

"Is that the same as me writing out a long chain of thought in the prompt?" Mechanically, almost exactly. Chain-of-thought prompting ("let's think step by step", 2022) came before chain-of-thought models. Three differences: the model writes the working itself, in the same pass, from its own map; it has been trained (layer 4) to write the kind of working that statistically lands on checkable-correct answers, so its working is optimised for retrieval-effectiveness rather than validity; and its version is cheap for you, billed to you, and unverified, where yours costs your time and is verified by construction. Context engineering and reasoning models are the same lever pulled from opposite ends.

"When it writes 'try route X, check capacity, violates B, try Y', is it using a calculator?" No. That is also generated text. After "route X carries 120 units and capacity is 100, so" the overwhelmingly likely next tokens are "this violates the constraint". The comparison is retrieved, not computed, the same way "27 × 43 =" is retrieved. Familiar numbers: usually right. Odd numbers, long chains, a new constraint shape: sometimes wrong, and nothing catches it. Two things to keep apart: at training time there was a real checker (the answer-verification pipeline above), which shaped the patterns once; at inference time there is no checker unless you add one. A product that calls a code interpreter or calculator mid-answer is a compound system (Rung 9): the model proposes the step, a tool computes it, the exact result is pasted back into the prompt. That part is exact, but it is harness, not model, and the model still decides by pattern when to call the tool.

Why step-by-step works at all. The space of whole problems is huge and sparse: your specific constrained optimisation has no near neighbour. The space of next steps is small and dense: "set up the constraint", "take the first-order condition", "compare to capacity" each appear millions of times across unrelated problems. Generating step by step turns one rare-neighbourhood retrieval into a chain of dense-neighbourhood retrievals, each with a good local match. Two consequences follow. The chain compounds error: ten dependent steps at 95% each is about 60% overall, at 90% each about 35%. And it collapses when even the steps stop matching, which is exactly what Mystery Blocksworld tests.

Kambhampati's three tests for reasoning that reasoning models fail: runtime that scales with problem difficulty (they generate correlated tokens, they don't search); explicit intermediate representations that are created and manipulated (their traces are appended text, and can be invalid); and an external checker that can validate the process (only the final answer can be checked, and only where a verifier exists). Meursault's version is Kahneman: a vanilla LLM is pure System 1, a reasoning model is marketing's claim to System 2, "it just tries really hard". His test is Kosuth's Chairs: a chair, a photograph of a chair, a dictionary definition of a chair. All three are called a chair; you can sit on one. For reasoning, sitting on it means generalising to an instance the model has never seen.

Costs: more test-time compute buys performance at the price of latency, inference cost and more places for an intermediate mistake to redirect the answer. Thinking tokens are billed, which is why "instant" mode became the exception and "Answer now" a toggle: not a conspiracy, a convenient alignment of the technical response to Blocksworld with the revenue line.

Constraints, Lagrangians, and why the solver still does the work AIBS

The instinct that "a constrained problem has a correct answer, so how can statistically-likely text solve it?" is right, and it is the argument. A reasoning model does not solve the constrained problem; it gets better at retrieving solution-shaped patterns. Nothing inside the model enforces the constraint. So the model is a good heuristic for proposing a candidate, and the thing that makes the answer correct has to sit outside it: the LLM proposes, the Lagrangian disposes. Where a solver exists, use the solver; the reasoning model earns its keep only where no solver exists, which is most business tasks, because they are not formalised enough to have one, and there it is an imperfect proposer that needs critics.

Reasoning, defined. Finding actions that move the world from the current state to an acceptable goal state, with the whole path inside the constraints. A business reasoning task asks for a goal to be achieved, not an answer to be given: a shipment plan has a goal (meet demand), actions (allocate shipments), constraints (capacity, inventory, timing, budget) and uncertainty (demand, delays). Formalised, that is a state space, actions, action costs, a goal and constraints, and reasoning is search for an action sequence that reaches the goal without violating them. Maths and business share the structure; business is just less formal, and still needs an explicit definition of acceptable output.

LLM-Modulo (Kambhampati, written before reasoning models existed) separates proposing from judging: (1) the LLM proposes a candidate; (2) hard critics test formal constraints (a solver checks feasibility); (3) soft critics assess practical quality (a manager reviews the plan); (4) a controller turns the failures into feedback and re-prompts; (5) the LLM proposes a revised candidate. The loop improves candidates because "good" is defined outside the model. The question stops being "can this model reason?" and becomes "can it generate useful candidates for external verification?" Generalised, a reliable AI workflow needs four things: context (domain rules, company data, examples, the requested outcome), a candidate generator, critics (software for formal constraints, people and tools for softer requirements) and control. Agentic coding tools are this pattern in the wild: the compiler and the test suite are the hard critics.

The economist's advantage, per Meursault: the labs are improving the proposer; the verifiers are domain-specific and nobody at a lab is going to build yours. Hard verifiers where possible, soft verifiers where necessary.

What actually moves the output: coordinates, documents, extraction, verification AIBS

Two different additions do two different jobs. Adding characteristics (task, audience, use, constraints, an example) changes the prompt's coordinates: "write a social post" lands in a generic region; "for Taylor Swift fans who don't follow football, explain why the win mattered" moves it toward accessible fan explanations. Adding a source (the briefing, the annual report, the spec) changes what information is directly available in the context. You do not need to specify every word of the answer; you need enough coordinates to reach the right neighbourhood, and adherence to a supplied source is far easier to verify than an abstract quality. Context changes how likely the output looks, not how correct it is.

Why "make it extraction" works. Ask "what were SmallCo's revenues?" and the model retrieves from its memory of similar-looking texts, where the rare fact loses. Attach the report and ask "find the revenue figure in this document and cite the passage" and the answer is in the prompt, the nearest documents are the ones you supplied, and the task is "copy the right span out of a given text", which the training data contains millions of examples of and which you can check by reading the cited passage. Any business question that can be rephrased as "pull X out of Y" should be.

User stories are the practical instrument. "When [who] does [action], you must [X]; you must not [Y]." A word like "proactive" collapses a few thousand behaviours into one token and the model picks one; enough stories surface the recurring whos, whens and musts that define the product. You cannot put the business goal itself ("more followers") in the prompt, because that only moves you to some engagement region; you have to specify what good looks like. Write stories so that future verification is fast, and use the model to turn accepted outputs into reusable prompts (meta-prompting).

Verification, restated. The documents behind an answer are permanently invisible, so verification can never mean checking the sources. It means checking the output against the stories, constraints and documents you supplied, or against an external ground truth where one exists (code runs, a database, a solver). The leadership version: do not only optimise the prompt; see the system, operate the loop, update the system.

RAG versus thinking tokens, since they get confused. Both augment the prompt before the response. RAG is a separate non-LLM retriever (usually semantic search over your own documents in a vector database) pasting identifiable documents in from outside; thinking tokens are the model's own generated working, from inside, with no recoverable sources. For a factual question about a document you hold, RAG or simply attaching the document wins every time.

The economics: where the money pools and why

The one equation that runs the industry

Total cost = F (training, fixed) + V × Q (inference: cost per query × volume)

Training (F) is a fixed cost, paid once per model generation, before a single customer pays anything: hundreds of millions to $1bn+ per frontier run as of 2026, and rising steeply each generation. Inference (V×Q) is the operating cost that scales with every message, forever. (The marginal cost economists keep invoking is just V: what one extra query costs to serve. Roughly: fractions of a cent for a short chat answer, up to dollars for a long agent task, as of 2026 est.) This split is the industry's whole financial personality: enormous fixed bets up front, then a margin race on serving costs. High fixed costs + low marginal costs is the classic recipe for concentrated markets (software, semiconductors, pharma), and that is exactly what has formed: 3-4 frontier labs, 3 big clouds, 1 dominant chip designer, 1 leading fab, 1 lithography monopoly.

The model-vintage view: every model is its own little company

The most clarifying frame in the industry comes from Dario Amodei (Anthropic CEO, Dwarkesh podcast, Feb 2026): treat each model generation as a standalone business. Train it for $X; over the next year or so it earns revenue serving customers at 50-75% gross margin; on its own profit-and-loss account (P&L) it is profitable. The company around it still loses money, because while model N earns, the lab is already spending several times more training model N+1. In his stylised example: a model trained last year for $1bn produces $4bn of revenue this year at $1bn of serving cost: 75% gross margin, $3bn profit on that vintage. Yet the lab still ends the year around breakeven or in the red, because this year it is also spending ~$3bn training the successor, plus billions more on researchers, experiments that never ship, and salaries.

Why keep doing it? Stop training and you would be profitable for a few months, then irrelevant: margin comes from being at the frontier, and the frontier moves. This is research-treadmill economics, like pharma pipelines, not a price war: the cost of staying differentiated rises every generation, while each generation individually pays for itself. Amodei's stated path: profitable by ~2028 (or earlier, he hedges, if revenue keeps compounding).

Bertrand vs Cournot: why the layers behave differently

Two textbook competition models explain most of this industry's pricing:

Bertrand (price competition on identical products): if buyers only care about price and entry is easy, sellers undercut each other until price hits marginal cost. Profit evaporates. This is the gravity acting on low-barrier, identical-product layers: GPU-only rental, server assembly, undifferentiated API resellers.

Cournot (few players, capacity constraints, hard entry): when only 3-4 firms can credibly play, because entry needs tens of billions in capital and rare expertise, each firm rationally limits how hard it competes, and prices settle above marginal cost without any collusion. Margins are "not astronomical, but not zero".

Dario Amodei made exactly this distinction about AI (Dwarkesh, Feb 2026): "Cloud is very undifferentiated... models are more differentiated than cloud", and on lab competition: "we have the... Cournot equilibrium... If there's like three firms... it doesn't equilibrate to zero." The neocloud knife-fight is Bertrand; the frontier-lab and hyperscaler layers are Cournot; and the monopoly layers (ASML, TSMC at the edge, Nvidia for now) price however they choose, constrained mainly by politics and long-term strategy.

Barriers to entry are a third escape from commoditisation that "differentiate or be cheapest" framings miss: sheer capital and expertise walls keep the big layers concentrated even where products converge.

Three possible endgames for the model layer (the "equilibria")

From the vault's AI Economic model note, synthesising Amodei's framing. Which one lands is the question.

1. Commodity. Capabilities converge (open-weight models catch up; frontier labs reach parity). Price falls toward marginal inference cost; training becomes unrecoverable sunk cost; value flows to whoever owns distribution (Microsoft's Office seats, Google's Search box). The labs' nightmare.

2. Winner-takes-most. A persistent capability gap plus rising switching costs (accumulated context, integrations, agent harnesses) gives the leader enterprise-software economics: entrenched, high-margin. What OpenAI and Anthropic are betting their investors' billions on.

3. Vertical capture. The hyperscalers, controlling the compute underneath, extract the rent whichever model wins; labs become brilliant, structurally dependent R&D shops. The circular investment deals (Microsoft→OpenAI, Amazon+Google→Anthropic) hedge exactly this outcome.

Mid-2026 evidence leans toward 2, with 3 lurking: labs are repricing on value delivered and margins are expanding. But one great open-weight release could snap it back toward 1. See Live debates.

Where the value went, and where it's going

2023-25: infrastructure ate first. Nvidia ran ~75% gross margins at peak; memory vendors' stocks tripled; power utilities became AI trades (Vistra +265% in 2024). The labs, meanwhile, burned cash and subsidised usage to win share.

Late 2025: the inflection. Agentic workloads started completing real multi-step work. The value of a query jumped from "better search result" to "hours of skilled labour", and with it what customers will pay.

2026: value migrating to the model labs (the SemiAnalysis thesis, May 2026). Lab revenues are compounding ~10x/year while serving margins expand (38% → 70%+ per SemiAnalysis, as Blackwell-generation hardware multiplied throughput). Labs increasingly price on value, not cost, because frontier capability has no close substitute and demand exceeds supply.

Latest, Aug 2026: Anthropic's annualised revenue reached $65bn in August 2026, up from about $47bn two months earlier, an $18bn increase in roughly eight weeks. That is still consistent with the 10x-a-year framing, if anything ahead of it.

The strategic puzzle: the monopolists are underpricing. Nvidia and TSMC both have demand far above supply and could charge materially more. SemiAnalysis's hypothesis: Nvidia acts as the "central bank of AI", holding prices below the ceiling to avoid antitrust heat, keep the ecosystem healthy, and maximise its long-run position. The sceptics' alternative (Zvi Mowshowitz): Jensen Huang simply runs Nvidia as an ordinary hardware business and does not price in what the chips actually enable. Same fact, two readings; either way, the rent they leave on the table is currently being collected one layer up, by the labs.

The investment paradox (Amodei): if you truly believed transformative AI was 2 years away, you would buy trillions of dollars of compute; nobody does, because being early is fatal: "If you're off by only a year, you destroy yourselves" (Dwarkesh podcast, Feb 2026). Even the biggest believers size their bets to survive being wrong about timing: one reason supply keeps undershooting demand.

Live debates: the questions the industry is actually arguing about

Settled facts live in the other tabs. These are the open questions, presented with the strongest case on each side and what would settle them. Positions here are contested by smart, informed people; hold them loosely.

Debate 1 Does compute commoditise?

The commoditisation case. An H100-hour is an H100-hour: identical product, dozens of sellers, easy entry at the bottom. Textbook Bertrand conditions. The evidence: neoclouds already price at a third of hyperscaler list, their marketing is interchangeable, and the 3-8x price spread for identical hardware looks like froth waiting to compress. On this view, owning a thin GPU-rental slice of the stack is standing where the price war will be fought.

The counter-evidence, mid-2026. Rental prices rose ~38% from late 2025 into 2026 (1-yr H100 contracts, SemiAnalysis index): scarcity is currently beating commoditisation because demand (agents, reasoning models) is growing faster than power-constrained supply. And the hyperscaler premium is not all froth: data gravity, enterprise integration and regulatory compliance certifications are real switching costs. Chips also depreciate fast, so today's glut candidate is obsolete in 3 years.

What to watch: the SemiAnalysis rental index when supply finally catches demand; whether any neocloud besides CoreWeave escapes spot-market pricing into long contracts; hyperscaler-vs-neocloud spread compression.

Debate 2 Does the CUDA moat hold?

Moat-is-deep: 20 years of tooling, every framework optimised for CUDA first, annual hardware cadence, and the whole-rack strategy moving competition to a level where Nvidia has no peer. Even well-funded rivals' software (AMD ROCm) remains years behind in practice.

Moat-is-narrowing: the biggest buyers are the least locked in. Google trains and serves on TPUs; Anthropic runs on TPUs and Trainium at scale (Jensen Huang publicly frames this as a one-off: "It's 100% Anthropic"; sceptics read it as the start of a structural break). Inference, the growing share of demand, is far more substitutable than training. And the moat's strangest feature (the observation of Zvi Mowshowitz, a widely read independent AI analyst): Nvidia's moat against Google is, mostly, Google itself, because Google could sell TPUs to outsiders at scale but chooses not to.

Update, Aug 2026: SemiAnalysis published an open benchmark testing whether Nvidia's CUDA advantage survives in agentic, multi-turn inference with long context and heavy KV-cache reuse, the workload pattern now displacing simple chat. Separately, OpenAI's self-reported results for its first inference chip, Jalapeño, claim to beat current Nvidia hardware on tokens per user and throughput per kilowatt, though this is not independently verified. Neither result settles the debate, but both give it, for the first time, a concrete empirical testbed rather than just architecture arguments.

Further update, Sep 2026: Google began opening its TPU inference stack (Ironwood, TPUv8i) to outside customers through a new offering, InferenceX, with SemiAnalysis reporting claims of up to 50% better performance per dollar than the alternative (SemiAnalysis, Sep 2026). If that holds up at scale, it is the first time Google has offered its cost advantage to customers who are neither Google nor Anthropic, giving cloud buyers a credible non-Nvidia option for inference specifically, the workload this debate already flags as the more substitutable half of the moat.

What to watch: whether Google ever sells TPUs externally at scale; Trainium 3/4 adoption beyond Anthropic; what share of new frontier training runs are non-Nvidia by 2027.

Debate 3 Do open-weight models cap the labs' pricing power?

Yes-case: DeepSeek, Qwen and Llama-class models sit 6-18 months behind the frontier at a fraction of the price, and agents (which burn tokens ~10x faster than chat) push cost-sensitive builders straight to them. If "good enough" keeps rising, the closed labs' premium segment shrinks toward a luxury niche: Equilibrium 1 by erosion.

No-case: for high-stakes enterprise work, the gap between "almost frontier" and "frontier" is worth full price (compliance, reliability, capability on the hardest tasks), and switching costs are rising as companies build agent harnesses, accumulated context and integrations around one model family. The line from Anthropic's own go-to-market team (the people who sell Claude to enterprises): consumer and casual use may commoditise; enterprise will not.

Update, Aug 2026: the gap is visibly narrowing. Alibaba's Qwen3.8-Max claims performance rivalling Anthropic, OpenAI and Kimi K3 and is being released widely; ByteDance is reportedly training a model three times the size of Kimi K3 aimed squarely at Anthropic's frontier systems; and SaferAI assesses Z.ai's open-weight GLM-5.2 as nearing frontier capability while missing the safety mitigations closed models have. This is the strongest evidence yet for the side of this debate that says open weights will cap what the closed labs can charge, though the safety gap SaferAI flags is a real cost that a pure price comparison misses.

Further update, Aug 2026: two more data points lean toward 'yes'. Meta itself now splits releases into an open-weight model (Glimmer) and a closed, more capable one (Muse Spark), suggesting even the industry's leading open-weight advocate keeps its frontier work closed. Separately, OpenAI and Anthropic both cut prices in August 2026, citing competitive pressure from Chinese labs.

Latest, Aug 2026: Anthropic's own flagship model, Fable 5, is reportedly struggling with enterprise adoption, with corporate buyers opting for cheaper alternatives instead. If accurate, this suggests price competition is squeezing pricing power even at the frontier, not just at the commodity end of the market.

Latest, Sep 2026: A Mozilla report, previewed by Ars Technica on 15 Sep 2026, finds that paying for a frontier model buys about a four-month capability lead at about five times the cost of cheaper open alternatives. This is one report, seen only in preview. If it holds, it weakens the labs' pricing power wherever the cheaper model is good enough. Enterprise vendors are already building on open weights: Salesforce and Nvidia launched Koa, a sales-focused reasoning model built on Nvidia's open-weight Nemotron (TechCrunch, 15 Sep 2026).

What to watch: enterprise win-rates and pricing of frontier vs open models each release cycle; whether any Fortune 500 runs core workflows on open weights at scale.

Debate 4 Is the capex boom rational, or a bubble?

Rational: revenue at the model layer is compounding ~10x/year, inference margins are already 50-75%, and every layer below is supply-constrained. On those numbers, hundreds of billions of annual capex is buying into visible, paying demand ("hard to see there won't be trillions in revenue before 2030", Amodei).

Bubble: most enterprise AI value remains unproven in customers' actual profit-and-loss accounts; the deals are worryingly circular (clouds invest in labs, labs spend it back on the same clouds, and both sides get to report the same dollars as growth); depreciation on a trillion dollars of fast-obsoleting chips has to be earned back by applications that mostly don't exist yet; and macro-level productivity gains are not yet visible in the statistics. If agent ROI disappoints, the whole 2026 revaluation partly unwinds.

Update, Aug 2026: fresh earnings support the "rational" side. Palantir posted about $1bn in Q2 2026 profit with US sales up 23% year over year and guided to further growth. AMD's data-centre revenue hit $6.7bn in the same quarter, up 107% year over year, as AI demand pulled revenue away from gaming. Neither figure settles the debate on its own, since revenue growth does not prove the capex is earning its cost of capital, but both are real monetisation, not just spending commitments.

Further update, Aug 2026: financing is now doing some of the work demand used to do. Nvidia is arranging roughly $500bn to fund AI data-centre buildouts, and private capital firms are structuring deals betting its chips keep their resale and collateral value for years. Read as bubble evidence: demand needs subsidising to keep pace. Read as rational: financiers are pricing in durable AI compute demand.

Latest, Aug 2026: a month of new evidence on both sides. For scale-is-real: Samsung is returning a record $80bn to shareholders in 2026 on AI-driven chip profits, and Alibaba raised $10.2bn in new shares to fund AI expansion after strong market reception of its Qwen 3.8-Max model. For bubble-risk: Nvidia is now arranging roughly $500bn in financing with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR to treat GPU compute itself as a bankable asset, on top of its own $100bn pledge to OpenAI's Ohio data centre and $1.5bn stake in the SoftBank-backed developer building it. Insurers will not fully cover the risk on the $14bn Meta-BlackRock data centre campus, and Citadel has sold about 80% ($4bn) of the AI stock portfolio it bought from Leopold Aschenbrenner's Situational Awareness Fund. Nvidia financing its own customers' data centres is a circularity pattern worth watching.

Further update, Aug 2026: two more developments add weight to the bubble side of this debate. Nvidia is increasingly financing the same companies that buy its chips, a reported $200bn in vendor financing that critics say blurs real demand from circular deal-making. Separately, the SEC has subpoenaed Wall Street banks over the near-collapse of the Situational Awareness Fund, the hedge fund built on aggressive AI-growth bets that needed a Citadel-led rescue in July 2026.

Further update, Sep 2026: new evidence pulls both ways. For bubble-risk: SemiAnalysis published a detailed look at how much of the roughly $11tn AI infrastructure buildout Nvidia itself backstops through investments and financing tied to its own customers, questioning whether its balance sheet can keep absorbing that exposure; Oracle's Larry Ellison moved to sell up to $7.5bn of stock even as investors worried the company had overcommitted to AI infrastructure; and JPMorgan halted lending to Leopold Aschenbrenner's Situational Awareness fund after it took billions in losses in an AI stock sell-off, a sign banks are growing warier of AI-linked trading risk. For rational: Jensen Huang forecast a further 70% revenue jump for Nvidia next year and directly rejected the idea that its infrastructure deals are circular. The disagreement is no longer only about future demand; it now shows up in who is willing to keep lending against it.

Latest, Sep 2026: money still flows, but the financing is getting more complicated. Large rounds are still closing. Crusoe raised $3.9bn at a $30.9bn valuation (TechCrunch, Sep 2026), and Nscale filed for a US listing valuing it at $35bn (FT, Sep 2026). Signs of strain grew, though. OpenAI projects negative cash flows of about $280bn through 2030 (FT, Sep 2026), and is reportedly weighing a funding round at a $1.2tn valuation (FT, Sep 2026, unconfirmed). Altman said on 12 Sep 2026 that a 2026 IPO would be ill-advised, and the delay makes investor appetite for SoftBank's planned $50bn SB Energy data centre listing less certain (FT, Sep 2026). Holtec pulled its IPO, its founder citing weaker sentiment towards data centres (FT, 17 Sep 2026). Texas is slowing data centre approvals as local opposition grows (FT, 13 Sep 2026). Net effect this week: more evidence on the financing side of the bubble argument, while large rounds still close.

What to watch: application-layer revenue (not lab revenue) as the honest scoreboard; utilisation and pricing on 2024-25 vintage GPUs; whether the circular deals get repriced by markets.

Debate 5 Do the scaling laws keep paying?

The worry: pretraining gains per dollar have visibly slowed ("we are near the end of the exponential", as the Dwarkesh-Amodei episode title puts it); high-quality training data is increasingly consumed; each generation costs several times more for smaller headline jumps.

The rejoinder: reasoning training opened a second axis (spend more compute per answer), agents opened a third (spend more compute per task), and capability per dollar at fixed quality still falls ~10x/year. The exponential may be ending in pretraining while compounding elsewhere. Amodei's own stated confidence, Feb 2026: powerful AI ("country of geniuses in a data centre") ~50% within 1-3 years, 90% within 10.

What to watch: whether the next full model generation (trained on Rubin-class compute) lands a step-change or an increment; inference-compute scaling results; data-efficiency research.

Debate 6 The geopolitical overhang

Two facts dominate: nearly all leading-edge fabrication sits on Taiwan, and the US-China export-control fight decides who gets frontier chips. US policy restricts ASML machine sales and Nvidia's China products; China pushes domestic alternatives (Huawei), estimated years behind at leading edge (Anthropic's 2026 policy estimate: US compute advantage ~11x). The industry's entire physical base assumes Taiwan stays open for business; that assumption is priced at roughly zero in day-to-day markets and would reprice everything at once if tested. Export controls are also a live commercial variable: policy swings (like the 2025 H20 China episode, where a US rule change left Nvidia's China chips unsellable: a ~$4.5bn accounting loss and ~10 points of gross margin gone in one quarter) move more money than most product launches. Two forces this page otherwise underweights also live here: controls are extending beyond chips to the model weights themselves (who may host and serve frontier models), and safety regulation is becoming a genuine industry variable, shaping what labs can ship, where, and with what disclosure.

Update, Aug 2026: both sides are tightening. Washington is reportedly drafting a ban on optical transceivers, the hardware that links AI data-centre networks, and China responded to earlier US sanctions with new drone export controls ahead of a planned Xi Jinping visit in September 2026. Separately, the FT reports early cracks in ASML's near-monopoly on advanced lithography as China narrows the gap: a longer-run risk to the tooling the whole industry depends on, not an immediate one.

Further update, Aug 2026: offensive AI moved from assistive to autonomous. China-linked attackers reportedly ran AI agents that conducted reconnaissance and intrusion against Taiwan with little human direction. OpenAI expanded its Daybreak programme with a model trained specifically for cyber defence in response.

Latest, Aug 2026: two more data points. Beijing is now letting leading Chinese firms buy limited quantities of Nvidia H200 chips, reversing its own earlier restrictions (FT, Aug 2026); and the UK government is urgently assessing the economic fallout after Donald Trump blocked foreign nationals from accessing Anthropic's Fable 5 model (FT, Aug 2026). Access policy, not only export controls, is now a live channel through which US decisions disrupt allied economies.

Latest, Sep 2026: the fight over frontier AI is widening beyond chips into model outputs and law. Anthropic published a report accusing Alibaba, Moonshot and DeepSeek of running distillation campaigns against its models (extracting outputs to train their own systems), and US officials separately accused six Chinese AI firms of aggressively copying US frontier models, urging American labs to identify Chinese users and quietly serve them weaker versions. South Korea broadened its espionage law for the first time in over seventy years specifically to protect chip secrets, and Huawei is reported to be coordinating a Chinese industry effort to build domestic DUV lithography tools, a step toward reducing reliance on ASML. None of this changes the underlying compute gap, but it shows both sides now treating model outputs and chip toolmaking, not only finished chips, as protectable national assets.

Latest, Sep 2026: should the frontier be paced? On 12 Sep 2026 Anthropic's Dario Amodei published an essay, "We Must Pace the Frontier". Anthropic: strengthening its position in the stack

Anthropic sits in the model layer and, increasingly, the tools layer (the API, Claude Code, Cowork). It owns neither of the things on either side of it: compute comes from AWS and Google, and the Amazon investment binds it to AWS; distribution increasingly comes through partners. The August 2026 Claudeforce deal put Salesforce's data, workflows and permissions inside Claude, with Salesforce expecting to spend around $300m on Claude tokens this year and holding a stake worth about $5bn, while saying plainly that the value it keeps is the data and the metadata. Anthropic's services venture, Ode (backed by Blackstone, Apollo and others), sends teams into companies and is the one route where Anthropic holds the client relationship itself.

Most defensible assetOwning both the model and the coding tool on top of it. Origin stories: one arc, five eras

The industry's history as a single story: first the ingredients accumulated (chips, data, a manufacturing base), then a proof that the recipe worked, then the architecture and the labs built on it, then the product that detonated demand, and now the industrial era the rest of this site describes. Knowing the arc, and the stories inside it, signals something no market-size number can. Each card ends with the line worth having ready.

The arc in one breath. Neural networks were an old idea that needed three missing ingredients: parallel compute (Nvidia's CUDA bet, 2006), labelled data at scale (ImageNet, 2009), and a manufacturing base (TSMC, 1987). In 2012 AlexNet proved the recipe; Google spent the decade's middle years proving it scaled (cat experiment, DeepMind, TPUs). In 2017 the transformer made the recipe industrial, and the labs formed around it. In 2022 ChatGPT packaged it, demand detonated, and the compute economy this site maps was born. Since 2025, agents and the DeepSeek shock define the questions still open.

Era 1 · The ingredients (1987-2011)

Nothing about AI's rise was sudden. The pieces were laid decades early, mostly by people mocked or ignored at the time.

TSMC: the customer who never competes (1987-)

Morris Chang, passed over for the top job at Texas Instruments in his early fifties, was recruited by Taiwan's government and founded TSMC in 1987, at 56. His heresy: a fab that designs nothing, so every chip designer on Earth can trust it with their blueprints. That trust model unlocked the fabless revolution (Nvidia could exist without ever building a factory) and made one company the manufacturing base of the entire digital world. Fuller story in the vault's Acquired TSMC note: the 1997 Jensen letter, the 40nm yield crisis, winning Apple.

The line "TSMC's product isn't chips, it's trust: the guarantee that your manufacturer will never become your competitor."

Nvidia's decade in the wilderness (2006-2016)

When Nvidia launched CUDA in 2006, spending hundreds of millions to make gaming chips programmable for general maths, Wall Street saw waste: the customers (a handful of researchers) barely existed. Jensen Huang kept funding it for a decade on conviction that parallel computing's day would come. AlexNet (trained on Nvidia gaming cards) was the vindication; by the time the AI boom arrived, every framework and every researcher already spoke CUDA, and the moat was twenty years deep. The company that nearly died in 1996-97 on a failed console chip became, briefly, the most valuable company on Earth.

The line "Nvidia's moat wasn't built during the boom; it was built in the decade everyone thought CUDA was a waste of money."

reCAPTCHA: the accidental data factory (2009+)

Those "prove you're human" tests Google bought in 2009 were a two-birds machine: while verifying you, your answers digitised books, transcribed street signs and house numbers, and later labelled photos ("select all squares with traffic lights" was literally training self-driving vision systems). Hundreds of millions of people unknowingly labelled Google's training data for free, daily, for a decade.

The line "Google industrialised data labelling before anyone knew it mattered: every CAPTCHA you solved was unpaid annotation work."

Era 2 · The proof (2012-2016)

Five years in which neural networks went from academic backwater to the thing every giant reorganised around.

ImageNet and AlexNet: the big bang (2009-2012)

Through the 2000s, computer vision was hand-coded rules and it barely worked. Fei-Fei Li at Stanford bet the missing ingredient was data, and built ImageNet: 14 million hand-labelled images (labelled largely via crowdworkers), with an annual recognition contest attached. For years entries improved by fractions of a percent. Then in 2012, three researchers from Toronto (Geoff Hinton and students Alex Krizhevsky and Ilya Sutskever) entered a deep neural network trained on two consumer gaming GPUs. AlexNet cut the error rate from ~26% to ~15%, a jump the field had expected to take a decade. Within two years every serious entry was a neural network, and within ten, the same recipe (neural nets + GPUs + huge data) scaled into ChatGPT.

The epilogue is even better: Google, Microsoft, Baidu and DeepMind bid at auction for Hinton's three-person company, DNNresearch, at the NeurIPS conference that December; Google won at $44m (announced early 2013). Ilya Sutskever later co-founded OpenAI and was chief scientist behind GPT; the "two gaming GPUs" detail is why Nvidia rules the world.

The line "Modern AI has one founding moment: 2012, when a neural net on two gaming GPUs won ImageNet. Everything since, ChatGPT included, is that recipe scaled up a million-fold."

The Google Brain cat (2012)

The same year as AlexNet, Google Brain wired 16,000 processor cores into one network and showed it 10 million unlabelled YouTube stills. Nobody told it what to look for; it taught itself a neuron that fired for cat faces. Trivial-sounding, profound in implication: neural networks could learn structure from raw data without humans labelling anything. It convinced Google's leadership to fund deep learning seriously, years before rivals.

The line "Google's cat experiment is why they were first: they had proof deep learning worked at scale a full ChatGPT-decade before the public did."

DeepMind and move 37 (2014-2016)

Google bought a ~75-person London lab with no product for a reported ~$500m+ in 2014. Two years later DeepMind's AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol at Go, a game with more positions than atoms in the universe, thought a decade away from falling. In game two it played "move 37", a move professional commentators initially called a mistake, then recognised as brilliant: the moment machines showed something that looked like intuition rather than calculation. It made AI a boardroom topic worldwide, and made DeepMind the research core Google later merged with Brain to build Gemini.

The line "AlphaGo's move 37 is when AI stopped being pattern-matching in the public imagination: a machine made a move no human would, and it was right."

The TPU decision (2013-2015)

Google calculated that if every Android user used voice search a few minutes a day, serving it on CPUs would require doubling its global data-centre footprint. Larry Page personally approved a ~$130m order for 40,000 Nvidia GPUs, and, in parallel, a 15-month crash programme to design Google's own AI chip. The TPU shipped in 2015-16 and has run Google's AI ever since: the founding act of the custom-silicon movement every hyperscaler now copies, and the reason Google's cost position is structurally different (see Debate 2).

The line "The TPU exists because Google did the maths on voice search and found buying compute would cost more than inventing a chip."

Era 3 · The architecture and the labs (2017-2021)

One paper created the modern model; the fights over who should control what it enables created the modern labs.

The transformer exodus (2017-)

Google invented the architecture behind everything (see The technology, Rung 5), published it openly, then moved cautiously while others industrialised it. All eight authors of "Attention Is All You Need" eventually left; several founded companies (Cohere, Character.AI among them). OpenAI took the blueprint and built GPT. It repeats Google's own earlier pattern: invent distributed computing, watch AWS build the cloud business on the open clone.

The line "Google invented the transformer and let it walk out the door: the defining strategic fumble of the era, and the reason 'code red' happened in 2022."

OpenAI: nonprofit to juggernaut (2015-)

Founded 2015 as a nonprofit by Sam Altman, Elon Musk, Ilya Sutskever and others, explicitly to keep AGI from being monopolised. Musk left in 2018 after a control dispute; the pure-nonprofit model could not fund frontier compute, so 2019 brought the "capped-profit" restructure and Microsoft's first $1bn. The 2023 board crisis (Altman fired and reinstated inside a week) previewed the governance tensions the field still carries; the ChatGPT story itself is Era 4's.

The line "ChatGPT wasn't a research breakthrough, it was a packaging breakthrough: GPT-3.5 existed for months; the chat box changed the world."

Anthropic: the safety schism (2021-)

Dario Amodei (OpenAI's VP of research) and his sister Daniela led a group of senior OpenAI researchers out in 2021 over differences on safety and commercialisation pace, founding Anthropic as a public-benefit corporation. The bet: you can only make frontier AI safe from the frontier, so build a lab where safety research and capability share one roof (constitutional AI, interpretability). Five years on it is the enterprise-and-agents leader, backed by ~$33bn from Amazon and up to $40bn from Google, valued at $965bn post-money by May 2026, running on all three silicon stacks, with revenue compounding ~10x a year.

The line "Anthropic is what happens when the safety team believes the mission enough to build the competitor."

Era 4 · The product explosion (2022-2024)

The technology barely changed in November 2022; its packaging did, and the demand shock built the compute economy.

ChatGPT and the code red (2022-2023)

OpenAI shipped ChatGPT as a "low-key research preview": GPT-3.5 had existed for months, the chat box was the innovation. It reached 100m users in two months, the fastest consumer-product adoption recorded up to then, and forced every boardroom on Earth to form an AI position. Google declared an internal "code red", recalled its founders, and began merging Brain and DeepMind into the Gemini effort. The H100 shortages, the neocloud boom, and the hyperscaler capital-spending arms race all date from the following twelve months.

The line "ChatGPT wasn't a research breakthrough, it was a packaging breakthrough: the chat box, not the model, changed the world."

The compute land-grab (2023-2024)

Demand for GPUs outran supply within months of ChatGPT. Nvidia joined, then dominated, the trillion-dollar club; CoreWeave turned from crypto-mining sideline into a major cloud; every hyperscaler multiplied its capital spending and accelerated its own chip programme; power replaced silicon as the binding constraint. The stack this site maps (see Big picture) took its current shape in these two years.

The line "2023-24 is when AI stopped being a software story and became an industrial one: chips, power, land, and capital."

Era 5 · The industrial era (2025-)

Agents multiplied the value of a query; efficiency shocks questioned the moats; the open questions live in Live debates.

The DeepSeek shock (January 2025)

A Chinese lab spun out of a hedge fund released a frontier-class open-weight reasoning model, claiming its final training run cost ~$5.6m (a contested, narrowly-defined number, but directionally real efficiency gains under chip export controls). The market read it as "maybe you don't need the giant compute moat": Nvidia fell ~17% in a day, roughly $590bn, the largest single-day value loss in market history. The panic partly reversed (cheaper AI means more demand: Jevons' logic), but DeepSeek permanently changed the pricing conversation and proved export controls breed efficiency as well as delay.

The line "DeepSeek was the first time the market seriously priced the possibility that intelligence gets cheap faster than compute gets scarce."

Agents and the value shift (2025-2026)

Models stopped just answering and started completing multi-step work: writing code end-to-end, running analyses, operating tools. Value per query jumped from "a better search" to "hours of skilled labour", token demand exploded (an agent burns 10-100x a chat's tokens), and pricing power visibly migrated toward the model labs, whose revenues began compounding ~10x a year. Whether that value-shift holds is the live question the Economics and Debates tabs carry.

The line "Agents changed the unit of sale: labs stopped selling answers and started selling completed work."

Views worth holding (with their backing)

A view plus one supporting mechanism beats a survey of both sides, in a room. These are defensible, not consensus-parroting; the full arguments live in Live debates and The economics.

  • "Compute commoditises at the rental layer, not at the frontier." GPU-hours are Bertrand (identical product, dozens of sellers); frontier models are Cournot (3-4 players behind $10bn entry barriers). Dario Amodei's own framing. Different layers, different physics.
  • "Labs are healthier than their losses look." Each model generation is profitable on its own P&L (50-75% serving margins); the losses are the cost of the next generation. That's a pharma pipeline, not a broken business model. The bet is on the treadmill slowing before capital patience runs out.
  • "The CUDA moat is real but it's a training moat, not an inference moat." Inference is substitutable (Anthropic runs on TPUs and Trainium at scale); training is where twenty years of CUDA tooling binds. Inference is the growing share of demand: watch that seam.
  • "Value is migrating to whoever owns the workflow, not just the model." Agents raised value-per-query from a search result to hours of completed work; switching costs now accrue in the harness, context and integrations around a model. That's also exactly where PE portfolio work lives.
  • "In portfolio companies, never lead with cost-cutting." Leading with headcount reduction triggers organisational rejection; lead with value creation and the cost side follows. (Hanover's operating lesson, and it matches Anthropic's enterprise experience.)

Question drills

"Walk me through the AI value chain."

One breath: ASML makes the only machines that print advanced chips; TSMC manufactures nearly all of them; Nvidia designs the dominant AI chips and owns the software lock-in; clouds rack them and rent compute; a handful of labs turn compute into models; apps sell the models' output as products. Margins pool at the monopolies and lock-ins (ASML, TSMC, Nvidia, arguably the frontier labs); everything undifferentiated in between gets competed thin. (Then pick whichever layer they care about and go deep.)

"Is this a bubble?"

Both answers with a test beats either answer alone: capex is underwritten by real, fast-compounding revenue at the model layer and genuine scarcity below it, but some deals are circular and the application layer hasn't proven the value in customer accounts yet. The test I watch: application-layer revenue and 2024-vintage GPU utilisation. If those hold up, it's a buildout; if they crack, 2026 gets partly repriced.

"Which LLM would you deploy and why?"

Have a real answer with reasons, e.g.: Claude for agentic and coding work and anything regulated (best harness discipline, enterprise posture); GPT for consumer-facing breadth; Gemini when the client is Google-stack and cost matters; an open-weight model (Llama/DeepSeek/Qwen class) when data cannot leave the building or token costs dominate. The meta-point that impresses: model choice is increasingly a portfolio decision per workload, not a religion.

"How would you drive AI value in a portfolio company?"

Sequence, not tools: find the 2-3 workflows where hours are the cost driver (reporting, customer response, document handling); ship one visible value-creation win in 90 days with the people who own the workflow; never lead with cost-cutting (it kills adoption); fix data access as you go, not as a prerequisite programme; then scale what worked across the portfolio. Measure in the P&L, not in pilots launched.

"Why did AI suddenly work after decades of failure?"

The ImageNet story (Origin stories tab): the algorithms were old; what arrived around 2012 was enough data (ImageNet) and enough parallel compute (gaming GPUs) at once. AlexNet proved the recipe; scaling laws then showed the recipe kept paying; transformers (2017) made it parallelise perfectly; ChatGPT packaged it. Telling it as a story, with the $44m Hinton auction as the punchline, is worth ten definitions.

"Won't open-source kill the labs' pricing?"

Segment it: consumer and casual use, plausibly yes over time; enterprise, much less so, because the frontier gap on the hardest tasks, compliance, and rising harness/context switching costs are worth full price. The evidence to date: frontier lab pricing power went up in 2026, not down, even with DeepSeek-class models free.

"What would worry you most if you ran a lab?"

Not competitors: the treadmill. Each generation costs ~3x more, so the business only works while capability gains keep justifying the next run. A scaling stall plus capital fatigue is the real bear case; commoditisation is downstream of that, not the cause.

Glossary & sources

Glossary

Agent
An LLM run in a loop with tools (run code, search, edit files): it picks actions, observes results, and continues until a task is done. Burns far more tokens than chat.
AI accelerator
Any chip specialised for AI maths: Nvidia GPUs, Google TPUs, Amazon Trainium. The umbrella term market-share figures refer to.
Antitrust
Competition law: the rules against monopolies rigging or dominating markets. Why "buy every rival" and "agree prices" are not available strategies.
API
The pipe through which software talks to a model hosted elsewhere; labs sell API access priced per million tokens.
Approximate retrieval
Mental model for what an LLM does: the prompt is a point on an invisible map of language, and the answer combines the nearby training documents through next-token prediction. Explains capability (a neighbour exists for almost anything) and hallucination (wrong neighbourhood, rare fact outnumbered) with one mechanism. Kambhampati's term; see the "How models work" tab.
Arm
UK company licensing the instruction set (the command vocabulary) most mobile and, increasingly, data-centre CPUs use. Paid a royalty per chip.
ASIC
A chip designed for one specific job (e.g. Google's TPU): less flexible than a GPU, cheaper and faster for that job.
ASML
Dutch monopoly maker of EUV lithography machines, the only way to print leading-edge chips.
Backpropagation
The algorithm that works out how much each weight contributed to an error, so training knows which direction to nudge it.
Batching
Serving many users' requests through a GPU at once so the cost of streaming the model's weights is shared. Main lever behind falling token prices.
Bertrand competition
Price competition on identical products with easy entry: undercutting drives price to marginal cost and profits to zero. The economics of the neocloud knife-fight.
Capex
Capital expenditure: money spent building long-lived assets (data centres, chips, fabs), as opposed to day-to-day running costs.
Chain of thought
Working written out before the answer. As a prompting trick ("think step by step", 2022) the user asks for it; in a reasoning model the model generates it itself. Either way it augments the prompt so the final answer is retrieved from a better neighbourhood; it does not make any step valid.
Cloud
Renting computing from someone else's data centre by the hour rather than owning machines. The generic term; hyperscalers and neoclouds are kinds of cloud.
Commodity / commoditise
A product identical whoever sells it, bought on price alone, so sellers' profits get competed away. The fate every layer of this industry is trying to avoid.
FLOP
One floating-point operation: a single multiply or add on a decimal number, the atomic unit of the arithmetic a neural network is made of. FLOP/s is how many a chip does per second (an H100 manages about a thousand trillion at FP16); FLOPs, plural, is a total amount of work done. Training a frontier model is on the order of 10^25 of them.
Compute
Computational resources treated as one fungible input bought by the unit, measured in FLOPs (floating-point operations) or GPU-hours rather than in particular machines. The abstraction is the point: it lets scaling laws, capex forecasts and export controls all reason about a quantity of work rather than a pile of specific hardware. "More compute" = more chips running for longer, whoever built them. As a formula: useful compute = rated FLOP/s (at a stated precision) × utilisation × time. Rated FLOP/s is the chip's speed on paper, and it rises as precision falls (FP8 gives more operations per second than FP16, which is why vendor headline numbers quote the lowest precision). Utilisation (often 30-50% in frontier training) is the share of that speed actually achieved, limited by how fast memory and the network can feed the chip, which is why HBM and NVLink matter: they raise utilisation, not peak. Time is how long the job runs. Multiply out and you get the 10^25-FLOP thresholds that export controls and the EU AI Act use. For inference the unit changes to tokens per second per GPU, and the cost that matters is dollars per million tokens.
Content provenance watermarking (C2PA)
A machine-readable marker embedded in AI-generated text, images or audio so it can be traced back to the model that made it. Anthropic began embedding these in Claude output in August 2026 to meet EU AI transparency rules, using the C2PA standard. Platforms are starting to act on the data: Spotify will label AI-generated "Persona" artist profiles and exclude them from recommendations from mid-September 2026.
Context window
How much text a model can consider at once, measured in tokens (frontier models: hundreds of thousands to millions).
Cournot competition
Competition among a few capacity-constrained firms behind high entry barriers: prices settle above marginal cost without collusion. Amodei's model of the frontier-lab layer.
CPU
General-purpose processor: a few fast cores built for sequential tasks. Runs operating systems and ordinary software.
CUDA
Nvidia's free software platform (2006) for programming its GPUs. Two decades of tools and habits built on it are Nvidia's deepest moat.
Data centre
A building full of servers. Modern AI sites draw hundreds of megawatts to gigawatts of power.
Data gravity
Data attracts compute: once your data lives on one cloud, running everything else there is cheaper and easier than moving it (egress fees make leaving cost real money).
Depreciation
The accounting cost of assets wearing out or going obsolete. Brutal here: an AI chip loses most of its value in 3-5 years, whether used or not.
Distillation
Training a small, cheap model to imitate a big one's answers: most of the capability at a fraction of the serving cost.
EDA
Electronic design automation: the software chips are designed in. Cadence + Synopsys duopoly.
Embedding
A learned list of numbers representing a token (or image, or document) so that geometric closeness tracks similarity of meaning.
EU AI Act transparency rules
In force from 2 August 2026: companies must disclose when a user is talking to an AI chatbot rather than a person, and must label AI-generated deepfakes and other synthetic content. It is one of the first concrete enforcement milestones under the EU AI Act and applies to any company serving users in the EU.
EUV
Extreme ultraviolet lithography: printing chip features a few nanometres wide using light made by lasering molten tin droplets. ASML-only.
Fab / foundry
A chip factory ($20bn+ to build at the leading edge). A foundry manufactures other companies' designs as a contractor.
Fabless
A chip company that designs but does not manufacture (Nvidia, AMD, Apple): sends designs to TSMC.
Fine-tuning
Adjusting a pretrained model's weights on your own data to specialise it.
Frontier lab
A company training models at the capability frontier: OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, xAI (definitions vary at the edges).
Genomic language model
An AI model trained on DNA/RNA sequences that can design new genetic sequences, not just analyse existing ones. In Aug 2026, researchers used one to design and synthesise new viruses, the first demonstration of an AI model creating a novel organism rather than predicting properties of an existing one. It points to real potential for redesigning organisms for medicine and industry, and to real biosecurity risk from misuse.
GPU
Graphics processing unit: thousands of simple cores computing in parallel. Built for games; conquered AI because both are massive parallel arithmetic.
Gradient descent
Training's core move: repeatedly nudge every weight downhill on the error surface.
Gross margin
Share of revenue kept after direct costs of the product sold (before R&D, overheads). The margin ladder of this industry: Arm ~95%, Nvidia ~71%, TSMC ~59%, ASML ~52% (latest reported, 2025-26).
HBM
High-bandwidth memory stacked next to the GPU so weights can be streamed fast enough. SK Hynix leads; chronically scarce.
Hyperscaler
A giant general-purpose cloud: AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud. Hundreds of services, of which GPU rental is one.
Inference
Running a trained model: every query, every message. The industry's operating cost, scaling with usage forever.
Instruction set
The vocabulary of basic commands a processor understands (x86: Intel/AMD; Arm: everyone else). Software compiled for one runs on any chip speaking it.
Instruction tuning
Second training stage: instruction-and-response pairs teach the model to place a task prompt next to its solution on the map rather than next to other tasks. Turns autocomplete into something that answers. Does not add facts.
IPO
Initial public offering: a company listing its shares on a stock market for the first time.
KV cache
The stored attention state that lets a model avoid re-reading the whole conversation for each new token; the memory cost of long contexts.
Language map
The visual form of approximate retrieval: everything sayable is a point, similar meaning is nearby, a prompt has coordinates, and context moves those coordinates. An intuition built on embeddings, not a description of the computation, and it never yields a source list.
LLM
Large language model: a transformer trained on text to predict the next token, which at scale yields general capability.
LLM-Modulo
Kambhampati's workflow pattern: the LLM proposes a candidate, hard critics check formal constraints (a solver), soft critics check practical quality (a person), a controller feeds failures back, the LLM revises. Works because "good" is defined outside the model. Context, candidates, critics, control.
Marginal cost
What producing one extra unit costs (here: the compute to serve one more query). Competition on identical products pushes price down toward it.
MoE (mixture-of-experts)
Architecture activating only a few specialist sub-networks per token: huge stored capacity, modest per-token compute. Standard at the frontier now.
Moat
Whatever stops competitors copying a business: lock-in (CUDA), monopoly know-how (ASML), scale, switching costs. No moat, no durable profit.
Mystery Blocksworld
The planning puzzle Blocksworld with its familiar words replaced by random ones, so no memorised solution matches and only the rules in the prompt can help. Vanilla LLMs fall to about zero, reasoning models to roughly 30%, a simple planner stays at 100%. The cleanest test of retrieval versus reasoning.
Neocloud
A GPU-only rental cloud (CoreWeave, Lambda, Nebius): cheaper per GPU-hour than hyperscalers, thin-moat, often debt-financed against the chips.
Node (3nm, 2nm)
Generation label for how small a chip's features are: smaller is faster and more efficient. TSMC leads at 3nm/2nm.
Off-distribution
A problem that differs in task, length or format from the families the model was trained on. Reasoning gains shrink toward zero as you move off-distribution: extrapolation outside the support of the sample. More thinking does not extend the support.
Parameters / weights
The learned numbers inside a model (GPT-3: 175bn). Set during training, frozen during inference.
P&L
Profit-and-loss account: a business's revenues minus its costs over a period. "Profitable on its own P&L" = the unit makes money viewed in isolation.
Post-training
Everything after pretraining that shapes a model into a useful assistant: RLHF, reasoning training, safety tuning.
Pretraining
The expensive first phase: predict the next token across internet-scale text. Sets most of the model's knowledge.
Prompt augmentation
Kambhampati's account of what a reasoning model does: prompt, then generated reasoning tokens, then an augmented prompt, then the answer. The tokens are retrieval keys that need to be statistically effective, not logically valid. Same retriever, better query.
RAG
Retrieval-augmented generation: fetch relevant documents and paste them into the context so the model answers from your data, not just its training.
R&D
Research and development: the spending on inventing the next product rather than making the current one.
Reasoning model
A model post-trained to generate long private chains of thought before answering, trading inference compute for reliability (the 2024-26 wave).
Reinforcement learning (RL)
Training by reward rather than by example: the model produces an attempt, a reward function scores it, and the weights are nudged to make high-scoring attempts more likely. RLHF uses human preference as the reward; reasoning training (RLVR) uses a checker on the final answer. The reward is generic, so what is learned is whatever patterns correlate with it.
RLHF
Reinforcement learning from human feedback: tune the model toward answers humans rank highly. Made chatbots usable.
Run-rate
Current pace annualised: this month's revenue x 12. How fast-growing companies quote revenue (flattering but standard).
Scaling laws
The empirical regularity that capability improves smoothly with more parameters, data and compute; the industry's investment thesis.
Token
The unit models read and write: a subword chunk, roughly ¾ of an English word. Also the industry's billing unit.
Sunk cost
Money already spent that no future decision can recover: a finished training run, recouped or not.
Test-time compute
Computation spent while answering rather than while training: a reasoning model's thinking tokens. Buys performance on hard tasks at the cost of latency, inference cost and more chances for an intermediate error to redirect the answer. Billed to the user, which is why thinking became the default.
TPU
Tensor Processing Unit: Google's in-house AI chip (10+ generations since 2015-16), used for Gemini and rented via Google Cloud; now also powering Anthropic.
Trainium
Amazon's in-house AI training chip (sibling: Inferentia, for inference), built to cut AWS's dependence on Nvidia; Claude is partly trained on it.
Training
Setting a model's weights by gradient descent over vast data: months of thousands of chips, paid once per model generation.
Transformer
The 2017 architecture built on attention: every token's representation re-weighted by every other. The basis of all modern LLMs.
Transistor
The microscopic on/off switch chips are made of; modern AI chips carry tens of billions.
User story
"When [who] does [action], you must [X]; you must not [Y]." The practical instrument for moving a prompt to the right neighbourhood and for verifying the output afterwards. Enough of them define a product.
Verifier (hard critic, soft critic)
Anything outside the model that checks its output. Hard: software that tests a formal constraint exactly (a solver, a compiler, a test suite). Soft: a person or tool judging practical quality. A model's own "check" inside its reasoning is generated text, not a verifier.
Wafer
The 30cm silicon disc chips are printed on and cut from; leading-edge wafers cost ~$20k+ and yield several dozen big AI chips.
